Coursework — INTB 3355
Germany · Legal Frameworks · Oct 2026
Navigating International Legal Frameworks: Entering Germany's Renewable Energy Market

Analyzed the BGB civil code, Energiewende policy risk, FDI screening under the AWG, and German employment law (KSchG/BetrVG) for a hypothetical U.S. energy tech firm. The EEG has been revised seven times since 2000 and some revisions retroactively impacted existing project finance. Data models built without regulatory scenario planning are fragile.

The same lesson applies to enterprise analytics infrastructure: governance is not overhead, it is the product's credibility layer. This paper reframed how I think about the audit trails I build at Perry Homes.

Japan · Cultural Environment · Oct 2026
Culture Unlocked: Entering Japan's Technology and Data Market

Applied Hall's high-context/low-context framework and Trompenaars' cultural dimensions to Japanese enterprise technology sales, examining how nemawashi, the ringi system, and ascription-based hierarchy reshape every step of a U.S. tech firm's market entry. In Japan, a data platform however technically superior fails without the relational infrastructure first.

This flipped my assumption that the best-performing system wins. Context, trust, and timing matter as much as architecture. A direct, low-context data presentation style fails in a high-context environment where meaning comes from relationship, not explicit output.

Reflection — How this semester changed my thinking
  • Where I
    started
    Technical by default.

    I came into this course thinking in systems, pipelines, models, dashboards. Business was a wrapper around the data problem. Three internships of real-world experience, all domestic, all inside U.S. companies. I had never seriously considered what happens when the system you are building has to exist inside a different legal code, a different communication culture, or a different regulatory regime.

  • What I
    learned
    Systems are contextual.

    The Germany paper was the turning point. Solar developers who built revenue models on existing feed-in tariffs defaulted when rates changed in 2012. That is not a data problem. It is a scenario modeling problem, a governance problem, a risk architecture problem. The same kind of problem I face when building dashboards people make real decisions with.

  • Where I
    applied it
    Governance as communication.

    At Perry Homes I am not just building dashboards. I am building governance frameworks and audit trails across five-plus business functions. This semester gave me language for why that work matters: data infrastructure is a form of institutional trust. I started documenting decisions differently, with the stakeholder's context in mind, not just the technical specification.

  • How I
    developed
    Pipeline thinker to systems thinker.

    Before this course, I described a data system as a pipeline: input, transform, output. Now I describe it as an embedded artifact shaped by the legal environment it operates in, the cultural norms of its users, the regulatory constraints on what can be collected, and the institutional relationships that determine whether anyone trusts the output at all.

  • Where I'm
    going
    Global data infrastructure.

    Energy data is global. The pipelines I build will touch systems governed by EU taxonomy regulations, GDPR, country-specific grid compliance rules, and cross-cultural stakeholder relationships. This course gave me the foundation to not be surprised by any of that. The commitment going forward: before designing any data system, ask who it serves, what regulatory environment it operates in, and what it means for it to be trusted, not just accurate.
